Micros are now half of CME's index futures volume. MNQ vs NQ: when to switch.

MNQ hit 2.9 million contracts a day in October, up 74%. One rule tells you when to stay in micros, when to use the mini, and how mixing both cuts your fees for the same risk.

On 3 November, CME Group reported a record October: 30 million contracts a day, up 15% on last year. Among its equity-index highlights: Micro E-mini Nasdaq-100 futures (MNQ), at 2.9 million contracts a day, up 74%. Micro contracts now make up half of all CME equity-index volume. More traders size in micros, and many keep doing it long after their account outgrows them. This note settles MNQ vs NQ with one rule: when to stay in micros, when to switch to the mini, and why the best answer is often both at once.

Key takeaways

  • MNQ is exactly one tenth of NQ: $2 per index point against $20. Same index, same prices, same stop.
  • Size the trade in micros first, then swap every full block of 10 MNQ for 1 NQ. Risk stays identical; you pay one commission instead of ten.
  • The swap only pays if one NQ round turn costs less than ten MNQ round turns at your broker. The calculator below checks it with your own fees.

02MNQ vs NQ: the only real difference is size

CME's contract specifications put MNQ at $2 times the Nasdaq-100 index, with a minimum move of 0.25 points, so one tick is worth $0.50. CME also states that every Micro E-mini is one tenth the size of its E-mini counterpart, which puts NQ at $20 per point. Both track the same index. A 30-point stop is a 30-point stop on either.

What changes is the step size of your risk. At a 30-point stop, each MNQ risks $60 and each NQ risks $600. With NQ alone you can only risk $600, $1,200, $1,800 and so on. If your budget per trade is $1,000, one NQ uses 60% of it and two NQ would break it. Ten MNQ risk exactly what one NQ risks, so micros let you land within $60 of the budget.

03The split rule: minis for the bulk, micros for the rest

You don't have to pick one. Size the trade in micros, then hand every full block of ten to a single mini:

NQ = floor(risk budget ÷ (stop × $20))
MNQ = floor((risk budget − NQ × stop × $20) ÷ (stop × $2))

Your risk budget per trade is the dollar loss you accept if the stop is hit. On a prop account, take it from the distance to your floor, as in the prop firm position sizing formula. On a personal account, it is the share of the account you risk per trade.

The split always carries the same risk as the all-micro size, because ten MNQ equal one NQ point for point. The only thing that changes is the number of contracts you pay commission on.

At $450 the budget doesn't cover one NQ, so micros are the only way in. At $1,000 the split saves $6 a trade. At $6,500 it saves $60 a trade: 20 trades a month is $1,200 a month in fees for exactly the same risk. Fewer orders also means fewer partial fills to manage when the market moves fast.

The check behind it: each NQ in the split replaces ten MNQ, so the split saves 10 × MNQ cost − NQ cost per NQ. With the hypothetical fees that is $10 − $4 = $6 per NQ. If your broker charged $10 or more for an NQ round turn at the same $1.00 micro rate, the saving would be zero and staying in micros would cost you nothing.

Decision rule

  • Budget under one NQ's risk (budget < stop × $20): trade MNQ only.
  • NQ round turn costs 10× the MNQ round turn or more: stay in MNQ. Same risk, finer steps, no saving to chase.
  • Otherwise: use the split. Re-run it whenever the stop or the budget changes.

05When to stay all-micro even with a big budget

  • You scale out in thirds or quarters. One NQ can't be split into three exits. If your plan takes partial profits, keep enough MNQ in the mix to cover each exit.
  • Your prop firm counts contracts its own way. Some firms set separate caps for minis and micros, or count them at a ratio. Check your account's contract limit before you mix the two.
  • Your stop is wide. At a 100-point stop one NQ risks $2,000. Most budgets under that are micro-only by the rule above, whatever the account size.

FAQFrequently asked questions

What is the difference between MNQ and NQ?

MNQ, the Micro E-mini Nasdaq-100 future, is one tenth the size of NQ, the E-mini Nasdaq-100. MNQ is worth $2 per index point and NQ $20 per point. Both track the same index at the same prices, so a stop of a given number of points is the same on either; only the dollar risk per contract differs.

When should I switch from MNQ to NQ?

Switch once your risk budget per trade covers at least one NQ at your stop, which is the stop in points times $20, and one NQ round turn costs less than ten MNQ round turns at your broker. Below that budget, or when the NQ costs ten times the micro or more, stay in MNQ.

Can I trade NQ and MNQ at the same time?

Yes. Size the trade in micros, then replace every full block of ten MNQ with one NQ. The split carries the same risk as the all-micro size, because ten MNQ equal one NQ point for point, and you pay commission on fewer contracts. Check your prop firm's contract caps before mixing.

CME Group reported Micro E-mini Nasdaq-100 futures average daily volume of 2.9 million contracts in October 2026, up 74% year on year. All Micro E-mini equity index futures and options averaged 4.4 million contracts a day, half of CME's equity index volume.
